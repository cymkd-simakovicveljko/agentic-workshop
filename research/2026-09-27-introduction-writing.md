# Writing the workshop introduction

## Audience and goal

Engineers who know code, Git, and tests but are new to coding agents. The introduction should explain what changes beyond autocomplete, why their judgment still matters, and what they will try first.

## Sources used

- [Google Technical Writing: Audience](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience) recommends teaching the knowledge the reader needs but does not already have. It warns against assuming subject expertise and using unfamiliar idioms. Application: assume software experience, explain agents through their actions, and avoid an AI glossary at the start.
- [ONS: Plain language](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/content/writing-for-users/plain-language) recommends user-focused content, clear headings, short sentences, consistent terms, and active voice. Application: speak to the learner, use concrete actions, and keep paragraphs short.
- [Nielsen Norman Group: How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/) reports a 1997 web-usability study and recommends scannable, concise, objective writing. Application: descriptive headings, restrained emphasis, and no unsupported productivity promises. Its findings concern web usability, not learning outcomes in this workshop.
- [Prashanth Rao: A framework for technical writing in the age of LLMs](https://thedataquarry.com/blog/a-framework-for-reading-and-writing/) describes writing ideas first, then the outline, then the details. This is personal writing practice, not a controlled comparison of teaching methods.

## Evidence behind the 2026 framing

- [Karpathy’s February post](https://twitter.com/karpathy/status/2026731645169185220), retrieved through the read tool’s Nitter mirror, reports the dashboard task and his shift toward managing agents. His time comparison is a personal estimate, not an independently measured speedup. His qualifications about oversight and testable tasks are retained. The mirror labels February 25; Willison’s quotation is dated February 26, so the lesson uses the month.
- [Ryan Lopopolo’s OpenAI account, February 11](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/) reports an internal beta built without manually written code. It is a vendor’s account of a deliberately constrained experiment, not an industry-wide result. The lesson does not repeat its estimated tenfold speedup.
- [Simon Willison’s definition](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/what-is-agentic-engineering/) supports the explanation of agents using tools in a loop and the engineer’s continuing role.
- [METR’s February 24 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) says selection effects and time-measurement issues make its new experiment an unreliable measure of the current productivity effect. Do not use its early-2025 slowdown as a verdict on 2026 tools, or its later point estimates as proven gains.

The search result for a purported Karpathy Sequoia summary returned 404. It was not used. Search summaries were not treated as verified quotations.

## Editorial choice

The user requested a wider introduction about the shift in software work before the app exercise. Open with moving beyond autocomplete, give attributed 2026 examples, explain agentic engineering, then connect the opportunity and remaining engineering work to the course.

This replaces the earlier task-count opening. The theme is changing how engineers work, not declaring programming obsolete. The proposed benefits are opportunities, not promised gains. This framing has not been evaluated with learners and does not establish that the workshop’s app or exercises are ready.

## What remains to check with learners

Ask an engineer new to agents to read it and explain what the agent can do, what remains their responsibility, and what they expect to do first. Confusion on those questions is a reason to revise. No learner evaluation has run yet.
